# Part V (continued) — Research Craft

## C.5 Why this is in a technical curriculum

Everything else in this document makes you better at the work. This section is
about the surrounding skills that determine whether the work matters: choosing
problems, writing them up, presenting them, refereeing, and not fooling
yourself.

**A warning about this genre.** It attracts enormous quantities of low-quality
motivational content, and the search results for every query here are dominated
by productivity influencers and AI-generated summaries. Every entry below is by
a practicing researcher with real standing, and where the canonical piece of
advice exists only as text, that is said rather than papered over.

> **Hamming is the single most important item in this section**, and the
> observation that does the work is this: *most scientists never write down
> their field's important open problems, and therefore never work on one.* The
> Method Atlas exists partly to make that list easy for you to write.
>
> **The companion text is the 1986 Bellcore transcript** (the video is a 1995
> restaging) and the book *The Art of Doing Science and Engineering*.
>
> **Alon's "cloud"** — the phase where the project is not what you planned and
> not yet anything else — is the most useful reframing available for a
> multi-year research program, because it says the disorientation is structural

> **Honest gap: Peter Medawar's *Advice to a Young Scientist* and "Is the

> there is no video substitute. Likewise **Michael Nielsen's *Principles of
> Effective Research*** and **Karpathy's *A Survival Guide to a PhD*** are

> **McEnerney has the highest ratio of behavior change to runtime in this
> entire document.** His claim — that your paper's job is not to explain your
> ideas but to create value for a specific community of readers who have the
> power to accept or reject them — reframes the introduction, the contribution
> claim, and the response to reviewers all at once.
>
> **Peyton Jones's rule is the one to adopt immediately:** do not wait until the
> research is done to write the paper. Write it first and let it drive the work.
>
> **Companion texts:** Gopen & Swan, *The Science of Scientific Writing*,
> **American Scientist 78:550** — arguably the best six pages ever written on
> scientific prose · Whitesides, *Writing a Paper*, **Adv Mater 16:1375**, which
> is the real artifact; the video is a condensation of it.

> **Kapoor's talk is the most directly relevant item in this entire section.**
> Everything else makes you a better scientist in general; this one is about the
> specific way your work will be wrong. A taxonomy of **eight kinds of leakage,
> documented across 329 papers in 17 fields, with bioinformatics explicitly
> among them.** Forty-eight minutes, and it should be watched before your next
> pipeline rather than after. **Atlas S.2 is this talk turned into a checklist.**
>

> seed variance alone can manufacture an apparent improvement. **Barba's talk is
> the computational-science version** — a satirical checklist of the practices
> that make results unreproducible, by someone who publishes full reproducibility
> packages for her own work.

## C.13 The five to do first

1. **Kapoor, *Leakage and the Reproducibility Crisis*** (48 min). Not because
   it is inspiring but because it is about the specific way your work will be
   wrong, and the fix is a checklist you can apply this week.
2. **Hamming, *You and Your Research*** (44 min). The only talk here that treats
   "make original contributions" as a tractable problem with identifiable
   inputs.
3. **McElreath A02 and A07** (2 hours). Counting paths, and the fact that a
   control variable can make your estimate worse.
4. **McEnerney, *The Craft of Writing Effectively*** (82 min). The difference
   between a technically correct manuscript and a cited one.
5. **Peyton Jones on papers, paired with Winston on speaking** (two hours).
   The same skill pointed at page and podium: write first and let it drive the
   work; convey one idea and make them want the paper.
