# B.3 — Computer Science

## B.3.0 The filter

This is not a computer science degree. It is the specific algorithmic, geometric,
systems and inference material that someone inventing molecular methods actually
needs, and it was entirely absent from the first edition.

A warning before the list: **the full essential set here is around 590 hours,
which is larger than the rest of Part I combined.** Computer science is where a
methods person over-invests most easily, because every course looks relevant and
most are only adjacent. Section B.3.9 gives a ~130-hour core path. Take that
first, and return to the rest only when a specific problem demands it.

**Langmead's course has the densest hours in this entire document.** Fifty-five
short videos covering k-mer indices, Boyer-Moore, pigeonhole filtering, edit
distance DP, overlap and De Bruijn graphs — the exact machinery you must have
reimplemented before you can modify an MMseqs2 or Foldseek-class search, or
write a new aligner. Six and a half hours for all of it.

**Kindermann's computational geometry course is the sleeper.** Convex hulls,
sweep-line intersection, range trees and k-d trees, Voronoi diagrams, Delaunay
triangulation, 3D convex hulls. That is literally every geometric primitive in a
packing, SASA, or pocket-detection pipeline — Voronoi and Delaunay for protein
packing and cavity detection, k-d trees for neighbor lists, 3D hulls for
molecular envelopes. Eight hours, and it replaces a shelf of folklore.

**MIT 6.849 is the unexpected one.** Demaine on linkage reachability and
rigidity is the rigorous theory of the object a protein backbone actually *is*,
and the course contains protein-folding-specific lectures. Loop closure, inverse
kinematics and rigidity analysis all come from here.

**Crane's discrete differential geometry** gives you curvature, the
Laplace-Beltrami operator and discrete exterior calculus on triangle meshes —
the correct formalism for molecular surfaces, solvent-excluded-surface curvature
features, and the geodesic descriptors MaSIF uses in Atlas I.

**Johnson-Lindenstrauss is the theorem behind every embedding-based protein
search**, and lectures 11 and 12 are the provable machinery for random-projection
featurization and low-rank approximation. If you ever claim that a compressed

or refuted. Lectures 13–16 on compressive sensing are also the theory behind
sparse-sampling cryo-EM and NMR reconstruction.

video; the notes and projects are public but there is no recording.*

**CMU 10-708 is quietly one of the most relevant courses in this document and
almost nobody in protein design takes it.** Belief propagation, junction trees
and variational inference are the formal home of message-passing side-chain
packing — tree-reweighted BP and dual decomposition were applied to packing years
ago and then largely forgotten. Direct coupling analysis is a graphical model.
ProteinMPNN is a graphical model with learned potentials. Seeing all three as the
same object is a real advantage, and this is where you acquire it.

Hennig's *Numerics of ML* treats linear solves, ODE integration and optimization
as inference problems with error bars — directly relevant to uncertainty-aware
integrators and to Atlas family A's surrogate models.

**Matsui's ANN tutorial is forty-eight minutes that determine whether a
structure-embedding search over 200 million predicted structures is feasible on
one machine.** IVF, product quantization, HNSW, and the memory/recall/latency
trade-off surface. Watch it, then watch the Foldseek and Steinegger talks in
Atlas N, and you will see the 3Di alphabet and k-mer prefiltering as specific
instances of the general machinery.

**Demaine's 6.890 teaches you to *construct* a reduction, not cite one** — so you
can prove your new formulation of threading, packing or design is hard, or
better, find the restriction under which it is not. Parameterized complexity is
the follow-on that explains why side-chain packing is tractable when the
interaction graph has small treewidth, which is the regime real proteins occupy.

## B.3.10 BUILD — Module B.3

1. **Implement a k-mer index and an exact Smith-Waterman** from scratch. Then
   implement the pigeonhole filter and measure the speedup. You have now written
   the core of BLAST.
2. **Implement a k-d tree neighbor list** and compare to cell lists for a
   Lennard-Jones system. Measure where each wins as a function of density.
3. **Build a Voronoi tessellation of a protein** and use it to compute packing
   density and detect cavities. Compare to fpocket's output.
4. **Work through CMU 10-414 to the point where you have written reverse-mode
   autodiff and a CUDA kernel.** Then implement one SE(3)-equivariant operation
   in your own framework. That is the capability this whole module exists for.
5. **Profile one of your own production scripts** with the 6.172 methodology.
   Find the actual bottleneck — it will not be where you think — and fix it.
   Report the speedup.
